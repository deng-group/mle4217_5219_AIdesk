*   Thermodynamics (high_throughput/thermodynamics.md)

The convex hull is a geometric construct used in materials science to determine the phase stability of compounds, particularly for systems with more than two components. It represents the lowest-energy phases at different compositions.

Its construction involves:
1.  **Plotting Formation Energies**: The formation energies of all calculated compounds are plotted as a function of composition. For a binary system (A-B), this is a 2D plot, while for a ternary system (A-B-C), it's a 3D plot (a ternary diagram).
2.  **Identifying the Lowest-Energy Surface**: The convex hull is the "lowest-energy surface" that connects the points representing the most stable compounds. It is formed by connecting these points such that all other points lie above this surface.

Compounds that lie on the convex hull are considered stable phases at 0 K and are in thermodynamic equilibrium. Compounds that lie above the convex hull are unstable or metastable and will tend to decompose into a mixture of phases that are on the hull. The vertical distance of a point above the hull ($E^{hull}$) indicates the driving force for decomposition.

While strictly valid at 0 K, at finite temperatures, the concept can be extended by replacing formation energy with the Gibbs free energy to account for entropic contributions (Thermodynamics, high_throughput/thermodynamics.md).

**Answer**

The design and use of descriptors are crucial in machine learning for materials science for several reasons:

1.  **Compatibility with ML Algorithms:** Standard machine learning algorithms typically require fixed-size numerical input vectors. Raw materials data, such as a list of atomic species and their coordinates, is often not directly ingestible in this format. Descriptors transform this raw data into a suitable numerical representation (Features and Descriptors | machine_learning_I/features.md).
2.  **Quantification and Comparison:** Descriptors are numerical representations of materials properties that allow for the quantification and comparison of different materials. These can range from simple properties like composition or electronegativity to more complex quantities derived from calculations (Data Mining | high_throughput/data_mining.md).
3.  **Input for Prediction:** Once raw data is transformed into features or descriptors, these numerical representations are used as input to machine learning algorithms to predict material properties or behaviors (Features and Descriptors | machine_learning_I/features.md).
4.  **Foundation of Materials Informatics:** The design of effective features is considered a cornerstone of materials informatics, enabling machine learning to predict properties, identify promising materials, and accelerate materials discovery (Features and Descriptors | machine_learning_I/features.md, Data Mining | high_throughput/data_mining.md).

While classical machine learning approaches often represent a material using a fixed-length vector of features, it's important to note that "flattening" complex structural information into a feature vector can sometimes lead to information loss. However, descriptors are still essential for providing the structured input that many traditional ML models require (Introduction | machine_learning_II/introduction.md).

You can construct a BaTiO3 structure using the `ase.Atoms` object in Python. BaTiO3 has a perovskite structure with Barium (Ba) at the corners, Titanium (Ti) at the body center, and Oxygen (O) at the face centers of a cubic unit cell.

Here's how to construct it:

1.  **Define lattice parameter**: Set the lattice parameter `a`.
2.  **Define atomic symbols and fractional positions**: Specify the symbols for Ba, Ti, and O, along with their fractional coordinates within the unit cell.
3.  **Create `ase.Atoms` object**: Use the `Atoms` constructor, providing the symbols, scaled positions, cell vectors, and enabling periodic boundary conditions (`pbc=True`).

```python
from ase import Atoms

a = 4.0  # Lattice parameter in Angstrom

symbols = ['Ba', 'Ti', 'O', 'O', 'O']
positions = [
    (0.0, 0.0, 0.0),       # Ba at corner
    (0.5, 0.5, 0.5),       # Ti at body center
    (0.5, 0.5, 0.0),       # O at face centers
    (0.5, 0.0, 0.5),
    (0.0, 0.5, 0.5)
]
atoms = Atoms(symbols=symbols,
           positions=[(x*a, y*a, z*a) for x, y, z in positions],
           cell=[(a, 0, 0), (0, a, 0), (0, 0, a)],
           pbc=True)
```

This code snippet creates a single unit cell of BaTiO3 (Source 1). The perovskite structure is a common prototype in materials science, known for materials like BaTiO3 which exhibits ferroelectric properties and undergoes a phase transition from cubic to tetragonal below 120 °C (Source 4).

Alternatively, you can download a CIF file for BaTiO3 from databases like ICSD and visualize it using software like VESTA (Source 2).

The convex hull is useful for determining the thermodynamic stability of materials, especially in high-throughput materials discovery.

Here's why it's useful:
*   **Identifies Stable Phases** It helps to identify compounds that are thermodynamically stable at 0 K (or at finite temperatures when using Gibbs free energy). Compounds that lie on the convex hull are considered stable phases.
*   **Distinguishes Stable from Unstable** It clearly distinguishes stable phases from unstable or metastable ones. Compounds that lie above the convex hull are unstable and tend to decompose into a mixture of the stable phases on the hull.
*   **Quantifies Instability** The vertical distance of a compound's formation energy above the hull ($E^{hull}$) quantifies the driving force for its decomposition, indicating how unstable it is.
*   **Compares Formation Energies** It provides a visual and computational framework to compare the formation energies of many compounds across different compositions, identifying the lowest-energy phases.

This analysis is crucial for predicting which materials are likely to be synthesizable and stable.
